6.3 The Four Pillar Ingredients of an Oncology ABM

Ordinary differential equation (ODE) models treat populations as continuous averages. In reality, however, cancer is driven by three factors: individual differences between cells, competition for space, and direct interactions with their surroundings. To capture these biological dynamics in a computer simulation, Agent-Based Models (ABMs) rely on four core building blocks:

6.3.1 Agents and State Variables

Agents are independent computational entities representing individual biological actors (for example, tumor cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes, macrophages, or stromal fibroblasts). Each agent maintains its own internal state variables, which may include:

  • Spatial Coordinates: Position \((x, y)\) on a discrete lattice or continuous coordinate space.
  • Agent Type / Phenotype: Cell lineage (e.g., prey/tumor cell vs. predator/immune cell, stem-like vs. differentiated, sensitive vs. drug-resistant).
  • Internal Resources / Health: Energy level, cell volume, or damage accumulation (e.g., DNA double-strand breaks).
  • Lifecycle Status: Active/proliferating, quiescent, senescent, or flagged for death (apoptotic/necrotic).

6.3.2 Environment (The World)

The environment represents the physical tissue domain and biochemical landscape in which agents live:

  • Spatial Topology: Typically presented as a 2D or 3D grid. The boundaries can be reflective (rigid walls) or periodic (wrapping around edges so cells exiting the right re-enter on the left).
  • Physical Exclusion: Rules dictating whether a single lattice site can host multiple agents or enforces volume control (carrying capacity per cell).
  • Background resources: Gradients of extracellular nutrients (glucose, oxygen), chemokines, or therapeutic drugs that diffuse, decay, and are consumed by agents.

6.3.3 Interaction and Behavior Rules

Rules define how each cell behaves and makes decisions at every step of the simulation. Some examples include:

  • Movement: Random walks, contact-inhibited crawling, or chemotaxis toward nutrient sources.
  • Feeding & Metabolism: Extracting energy from the local environment or prey to fuel baseline basal metabolic costs.
  • Immune Attacks (Predation): Immune cells spotting cancer cells upon physical proximity, causing them to lock onto them and destroy them (cell lysis).
  • Reproduction (Proliferation): Dividing into daughter cells when internal energy exceeds a threshold and space is available.
  • Cell Death (Apoptosis): Cell death triggered by starvation (energy dropping below zero), immune destruction, or senescence.

6.3.4 Iterative Scheduling Loop

Time advances in discrete increments (\(\Delta t\)). At each time step:

  1. Activation Order: Living agents are activated. In asynchronous scheduling, the order is randomized at every step to prevent execution order artifacts.
  2. Action Execution: Each agent evaluates its local neighborhood, updates its energy, moves, interacts, and potentially reproduces or dies.
  3. Environmental Update: Diffusive fields diffuse/decay and renewable resources regenerate.
  4. State Census: Macroscopic summary statistics (e.g., total population counts, spatial clustering) are logged for analysis.

6.4 Hands-On Implementation Walkthrough: Tumour-Immune Dynamics in R & Plotly

In Chapter 1, we introduced the classic Lotka-Volterra system to describe predator-prey interactions between cytotoxic immune effector cells (\(E\), predators) and tumour cells (\(T\), prey): \[\frac{dT}{dt} = a\, T - b\, T E, \qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = c\, T E - d\, E\]

In continuous ordinary differential equations (ODEs), populations are assumed to be “well-mixed” and uniformly distributed across continuous space. However, real clinical tumors grow in complex, structured physical microenvironments where space and local biology govern survival:

  1. Spatial Clustering & Nodule Formation: Tumours do not disperse evenly; they originate as dense, localized nodules surrounded by healthy tissue and vascular beds.
  2. Contact Inhibition (Volume Exclusion): Cells require physical room to divide. Even if nutrients are abundant, cells tightly packed in the tumor core cannot proliferate until space opens up at the tumor margin.
  3. Chemotaxis (Directed Search): Immune cells do not wander blindly. Instead, they sense chemical gradients (chemokines) secreted by tumour cells, migrating preferentially toward cancer nests.
  4. T-Cell Exhaustion: Chronic antigenic stimulation causes cytotoxic T cells to become functionally exhausted, losing their ability to kill or proliferate.
  5. Therapeutic Rescue (Immunotherapy): Immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as anti-PD-1 / anti-PD-L1 antibodies) block inhibitory pathways, reinvigorating exhausted T cells and restoring immune control.

Here, we translate these Lotka-Volterra tumour-immune dynamics into an Agent-Based Model (ABM).

How to Run the Models

  • Running in R / RStudio: Run each R code chunk in sequence (or click the green Run button at the top-right of each chunk in RStudio) to simulate the tumor microenvironment and generate the figures.
  • Interactive Plotly Controls:

6.4.1 Model Formulation and Biological Parameters

Our simulated tumor environment contains three interacting components:

  1. Tumour Cells (\(T\), Prey): The cells start as a tight cluster in the middle of the tissue. They take up nutrients supplied by nearby blood vessels to fuel their basic survival, move into open surrounding areas, and multiply until they run out of room (contact-inhibited growth). If they run out of nutrients and energy (energy drops below zero), they starve and die from necrosis.
  2. Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes (\(E\), Predators): These cells patrol the tissue domain. They detect tumor cells via chemotaxis and attack on contact with probability \(p_{\text{kill}}\). Killing a target provides them with energy (target lysis) and triggers them to divide. After sustained killing, T cells reach an exhaustion limit, losing both their ability to kill (cytotoxicity) and their ability to divide (proliferative potential).
  3. Vascular Nutrient Field (Resource): A 2D grid representing oxygen and glucose delivery from the capillary microvasculature. Once a tumor cell feeds at a site, the nutrients are depleted and a countdown starts until nearby blood vessels replenish it.

6.4.2 Data Structures: Demystified for Beginners

If you are new to programming or agent-based modeling in R, two design patterns in our code will make your life much easier:

6.4.2.1 1. Why We Use an R environment (new.env())

In standard R, when you pass a data frame or list into a function, R creates a duplicate copy in memory whenever you modify it (this is called “copy-on-modify”). In an agent-based model where thousands of cells move, eat, and divide across hundreds of time steps, copying the entire world at every tick would make the simulation extremely slow!

An R environment (m <- new.env()) acts like a shared notebook or container box. When functions read and update values inside m, they modify the original data directly in place, ensuring our simulation runs at top speed.

6.4.2.2 2. Why We Use “Parallel Vectors”

Instead of creating hundreds of separate complex objects for every individual cell, we store agent attributes in synchronized, simple 1D vectors:

  • m$x[i] and m$y[i]: the \((x, y)\) coordinate of cell i.
  • m$type[i]: whether cell i is a tumour cell (1) or a T cell (2).
  • m$energy[i]: the remaining metabolic energy of cell i.
  • m$kills[i]: how many tumour cells T cell i has eliminated.
  • m$alive[i]: TRUE if the cell is currently living, FALSE if dead.

For example, cell number \(7\) is described simply by looking at index \(7\) across all vectors: its position is (m$x[7], m$y[7]) and its energy is m$energy[7].

6.4.2.3 3. Spatial Matrix for Volume Exclusion

To enforce contact inhibition (at most one tumour cell per site), we maintain a 2D integer matrix m$tum. If coordinate \((x, y)\) is empty, m$tum[x, y] == 0. If occupied, it holds the ID number of that tumour cell.

6.4.2.4 4. The Moore 8-Neighborhood & Periodic Boundary Wrapping

Cells can move or divide into any of their \(8\) surrounding neighbors (North, South, East, West, and the 4 diagonals):

(-1, -1)   ( 0, -1)   ( 1, -1)
(-1,  0)   [ Cell ]   ( 1,  0)
(-1,  1)   ( 0,  1)   ( 1,  1)

To prevent cells from “falling off” the edge of the tissue grid, we use periodic (toroidal) boundary conditions: a cell that walks off the right edge wraps around to the left edge, and a cell walking off the top wraps to the bottom. In R’s 1-based indexing, this wrap-around is computed cleanly with the modulo operator:

wrapped_x <- ((current_x - 1 + dx) %% grid_width) + 1

library(plotly)

# Numerical labels for our cell types 
TUMOUR <- 1L  # 1 represents a tumour cell (prey)
TCELL  <- 2L  # 2 represents a cytotoxic T lymphocyte (predator)

# Moore 8-neighbourhood coordinate offsets (dx and dy for all 8 surrounding patches)
DX <- c(-1L,  0L,  1L, -1L, 1L, -1L, 0L, 1L)
DY <- c(-1L, -1L, -1L,  0L, 0L,  1L, 1L, 1L)

# Bundle default parameters into a clean, reusable list
default_params <- list(

  n_tumour           = 60,    # Initial tumour cell count
  n_tcell            = 10,    # Initial patrolling T-cell count
  resupply_time      = 12,    # Steps for depleted nutrient to replenish
  de_tumour          = 14,    # Energy gained by tumour from nutrient
  tumour_div         = 0.06,  # Tumour division probability per step
  tumour_motility    = 0.05,  # Tumour migration probability per step
  de_tcell           = 12,    # Energy gained by T cell per tumour kill
  p_kill             = 0.50,  # Base kill probability on contact
  tcell_prolif       = 0.40,  # Clonal expansion probability after kill
  exhaustion_limit   = 6,     # Kills before T cell becomes exhausted
  recruit            = 1.0,   # T-cell recruitment rate per 100 tumour cells
  chemotaxis         = 0.60,  # Chemotactic bias toward neighbouring tumour
  therapy            = FALSE, # Checkpoint inhibitor therapy active?
  therapy_start      = 200,   # Step at which therapy is initiated
  therapy_kill_boost = 1.6,   # Multiplier for kill probability under therapy
  therapy_exh_mult   = 4,     # Multiplier for exhaustion limit under therapy
  seed               = 1      # Random seed for reproducible results
)

6.4.3.2 Step 2: Initializing the Model Environment

The initialize_model() function builds the tissue lattice, seeds the central tumor nodule, places patrolling T cells, and sets up the vascular nutrient grid.

initialize_model <- function(p = default_params) {
  set.seed(p$seed)  # Fix seed for reproducible scientific simulations
  m <- new.env()    # Create our mutable simulation environment
  
  W <- H <- as.integer(p$dims)
  m$W <- W
  m$H <- H
  m$p <- p

  # --- Seed the Central Tumour Nodule ---
  # In vivo tumors originate as compact masses. 
  # To mimic this, we calculate each lattice site's distance from the center, add a small amount of random noise,
  # and pick the nearest sites to form a naturally rounded cluster.
  cx <- (W + 1) / 2
  cy <- (H + 1) / 2
  all_sites <- expand.grid(x = seq_len(W), y = seq_len(H))
  distances <- sqrt((all_sites$x - cx)^2 + (all_sites$y - cy)^2)
  ordered_sites <- all_sites[order(distances + runif(nrow(all_sites), 0, 0.5)), ]
  
  nt <- min(p$n_tumour, W * H)
  tx <- ordered_sites$x[seq_len(nt)]
  ty <- ordered_sites$y[seq_len(nt)]

  # --- Seed Patrolling T Cells ---
  # T cells begin scattered randomly across the tissue microenvironment
  ncel <- p$n_tcell
  n_total <- nt + ncel

  # --- Populate Parallel Attribute Vectors ---
  m$type   <- c(rep(TUMOUR, nt), rep(TCELL, ncel))
  m$x      <- c(tx, sample.int(W, ncel, replace = TRUE))
  m$y      <- c(ty, sample.int(H, ncel, replace = TRUE))
  m$energy <- as.numeric(c(
    sample.int(2 * p$de_tumour, nt, replace = TRUE) - 1,
    sample.int(2 * p$de_tcell,  ncel, replace = TRUE) - 1
  ))
  m$kills  <- integer(n_total)   # Cumulative kills per agent (tracks exhaustion)
  m$alive  <- rep(TRUE, n_total) # Life status: TRUE = active, FALSE = removed

  # --- Spatial Matrix (Volume Exclusion) ---
  # Holds the agent ID of the occupying tumour cell, or 0 if vacant
  m$tum <- matrix(0L, W, H)
  m$tum[cbind(tx, ty)] <- seq_len(nt)

  # --- Vascular Nutrient Field ---
  # TRUE = nutrient patch available; countdown = remaining steps until replenishment
  m$fully     <- matrix(sample(c(TRUE, FALSE), W * H, replace = TRUE), W, H)
  m$countdown <- matrix(
    ifelse(m$fully, p$resupply_time, sample.int(p$resupply_time, W * H, replace = TRUE) - 1L),
    W, H
  )

  # History tracking vectors for population time series
  m$step        <- 0L
  m$kill_events <- 0L
  m$h_step      <- integer(0)
  m$h_tumour    <- integer(0)
  m$h_active    <- integer(0)
  m$h_exhausted <- integer(0)
  m$h_nutrient  <- integer(0)
  
  record(m)  # Take initial census
  m
}

6.4.3.3 Step 3: Helper Functions (Adding, Removing, and Finding Neighbors)

These concise helper functions manage agent lifecycle changes, boundary math, and therapy state.

# Check whether checkpoint inhibitor therapy is active at the current step
therapy_on <- function(m) {
  m$p$therapy && m$step >= m$p$therapy_start
}

# Dynamic exhaustion threshold (expanded when immunotherapy is active)
exh_limit <- function(m) {
  m$p$exhaustion_limit * (if (therapy_on(m)) m$p$therapy_exh_mult else 1)
}

# Dynamic kill probability (boosted when immunotherapy is active)
kill_prob <- function(m) {
  min(1.0, m$p$p_kill * (if (therapy_on(m)) m$p$therapy_kill_boost else 1))
}

# Add a newborn daughter cell or newly recruited T cell to our parallel vectors
add_agent <- function(m, type, x, y, energy, kills = 0L) {
  m$type   <- c(m$type, type)
  m$x      <- c(m$x, x)
  m$y      <- c(m$y, y)
  m$energy <- c(m$energy, energy)
  m$kills  <- c(m$kills, kills)
  m$alive  <- c(m$alive, TRUE)
  new_id   <- length(m$alive)
  if (type == TUMOUR) m$tum[x, y] <- new_id
  new_id
}

# Mark a cell as dead and clear its coordinate in the spatial matrix
remove_agent <- function(m, i) {
  m$alive[i] <- FALSE
  if (m$type[i] == TUMOUR) m$tum[m$x[i], m$y[i]] <- 0L
}

# Calculate the coordinates of all 8 neighbors with toroidal boundary wrap-around
neighbours <- function(m, i) {
  list(
    x = ((m$x[i] - 1L + DX) %% m$W) + 1L,
    y = ((m$y[i] - 1L + DY) %% m$H) + 1L
  )
}

6.4.3.4 Step 4: Tumour Cell Behavior Rules

At each time step, every living tumour cell follows four biological rules:

  1. Motility: With probability tumour_motility, it migrates into an unoccupied adjacent patch.

  2. Basal Metabolism: It consumes \(1\) unit of energy. If its energy drops below zero, it undergoes necrotic cell death from starvation.

  3. Nutrient Uptake: If glucose/oxygen is present at its patch, it takes up the nutrient and gains energy (de_tumour).

  4. Contact-Inhibited Proliferation: With probability tumour_div, it divides into an empty adjacent patch, splitting its energy with the daughter cell. If all \(8\) neighboring sites are occupied by other cancer cells, division is physically blocked (contact inhibition).

tumour_step <- function(m, i) {
  p  <- m$p
  nb <- neighbours(m, i)
  
  # Identify which of the 8 neighbouring lattice sites are unoccupied
  free <- m$tum[cbind(nb$x, nb$y)] == 0L

  # 1. Migration: crawl to an empty neighboring patch
  if (any(free) && runif(1) < p$tumour_motility) {
    k <- which(free)
    k <- k[sample.int(length(k), 1)]       # Choose one empty neighbor at random
    m$tum[m$x[i], m$y[i]] <- 0L            # Clear old position
    m$x[i] <- nb$x[k]; m$y[i] <- nb$y[k]   # Update coordinates
    m$tum[m$x[i], m$y[i]] <- i             # Register new position
    
    # Re-evaluate empty neighbors after moving
    nb <- neighbours(m, i)
    free <- m$tum[cbind(nb$x, nb$y)] == 0L
  }

  # 2. Basal Metabolism: expend 1 energy unit for survival
  m$energy[i] <- m$energy[i] - 1
  if (m$energy[i] < 0) {
    remove_agent(m, i)  # Starvation: cell undergoes necrosis
    return(invisible())
  }

  # 3. Nutrient Uptake: feed on vascular glucose/oxygen
  if (m$fully[m$x[i], m$y[i]]) {
    m$energy[i] <- m$energy[i] + p$de_tumour
    m$fully[m$x[i], m$y[i]] <- FALSE  # Nutrient patch is now depleted
  }

  # 4. Proliferation: divide into a free neighboring site (contact-inhibited)
  if (any(free) && runif(1) <= p$tumour_div) {
    k <- which(free)
    k <- k[sample.int(length(k), 1)]
    m$energy[i] <- m$energy[i] / 2  # Parent divides energy equally with daughter cell
    add_agent(m, TUMOUR, nb$x[k], nb$y[k], m$energy[i])
  }
}

6.4.3.5 Step 5: Cytotoxic T Cell Behavior Rules

Each living T cell acts as a patrolling predator:

  1. Chemotaxis & Movement: The T cell evaluates its \(8\) neighboring patches. If any neighbor holds a tumour cell, it steps toward it with probability chemotaxis (chemokine gradient guidance); otherwise, it performs a random walk step.

  2. Metabolism & Finite Lifespan: The T cell loses \(1\) energy unit per step. If energy drops below zero, it dies of senescence.

  3. Exhaustion Check: If the T cell’s cumulative kill count reaches exhaustion_limit, it becomes exhausted: it remains physically present in the tissue but can no longer attack or proliferate.

  4. Contact Cytotoxicity: If active (non-exhausted) and sharing a patch with a tumour cell, it attacks with probability kill_prob, destroying the tumour cell (cell lysis), gaining energy (de_tcell), and logging a kill.

  5. Clonal Expansion: Following a kill, with probability tcell_prolif, the T cell divides into two active daughter T cells.

tcell_step <- function(m, i) {
  p  <- m$p
  nb <- neighbours(m, i)

  # 1. Chemotaxis: sense nearby tumour cells and step toward them
  nearby_tumours <- which(m$tum[cbind(nb$x, nb$y)] > 0L)
  k <- if (length(nearby_tumours) > 0 && runif(1) < p$chemotaxis) {
    nearby_tumours[sample.int(length(nearby_tumours), 1)]  # Directed step toward tumor
  } else {
    sample.int(8L, 1)                                      # Random exploration step
  }
  m$x[i] <- nb$x[k]
  m$y[i] <- nb$y[k]

  # 2. Metabolism & Natural Lifespan
  m$energy[i] <- m$energy[i] - 1
  if (m$energy[i] < 0) {
    remove_agent(m, i)  # T cell dies of natural senescence
    return(invisible())
  }

  # 3. Exhaustion Check: exhausted T cells cannot kill or proliferate
  if (m$kills[i] >= exh_limit(m)) {
    return(invisible())
  }

  # 4. Contact Cytotoxicity: attack tumour cell at current site
  target <- m$tum[m$x[i], m$y[i]]
  if (target > 0L && runif(1) <= kill_prob(m)) {
    remove_agent(m, target)               # Lyse the tumour cell
    m$kill_events <- m$kill_events + 1L
    m$energy[i]   <- m$energy[i] + p$de_tcell
    m$kills[i]    <- m$kills[i] + 1L

    # 5. Clonal Proliferation: divide following antigenic stimulation
    if (runif(1) <= p$tcell_prolif) {
      m$energy[i] <- m$energy[i] / 2     # Parent splits energy with daughter T cell
      add_agent(m, TCELL, m$x[i], m$y[i], m$energy[i], m$kills[i])
    }
  }
}

6.4.3.6 Step 6: Environmental Regeneration and Systemic Recruitment

Between cellular actions, vascular nutrients replenish and new immune cells enter the tissue from systemic circulation in proportion to tumour antigen load:

# Nutrient Regrowth: Depleted vascular patches count down and replenish
nutrient_step <- function(m) {
  R <- m$p$resupply_time
  growing <- !m$fully
  ready   <- growing & m$countdown <= 0
  m$fully[ready]     <- TRUE
  m$countdown[ready] <- R
  dec <- growing & !ready
  m$countdown[dec]   <- m$countdown[dec] - 1L
}

# Systemic T-Cell Recruitment:
# Bloodborne T cells extravasate into tissue at a rate proportional to tumour antigen load.
# The number of new cells arriving each step is sampled from a Poisson distribution.
recruit_tcells <- function(m) {
  n_tum <- sum(m$alive & m$type == TUMOUR)
  n_new <- rpois(1, m$p$recruit * n_tum / 100)
  for (j in seq_len(n_new)) {
    add_agent(
      m, TCELL,
      sample.int(m$W, 1), sample.int(m$H, 1),
      sample.int(2 * m$p$de_tcell, 1) - 1
    )
  }
}

# Census function: tallies active, exhausted, and tumour populations
counts <- function(m) {
  a   <- m$alive
  tc  <- a & m$type == TCELL
  exh <- tc & (m$kills >= exh_limit(m))
  c(
    tumour    = sum(a & m$type == TUMOUR),
    active    = sum(tc & !exh),
    exhausted = sum(exh),
    nutrient  = sum(m$fully)
  )
}

# History logger for time-series trajectories
record <- function(m) {
  cc <- counts(m)
  m$h_step      <- c(m$h_step, m$step)
  m$h_tumour    <- c(m$h_tumour, cc[["tumour"]])
  m$h_active    <- c(m$h_active, cc[["active"]])
  m$h_exhausted <- c(m$h_exhausted, cc[["exhausted"]])
  m$h_nutrient  <- c(m$h_nutrient, cc[["nutrient"]])
}

6.4.3.7 Step 7: The Master Scheduling Loop

At each discrete simulation step (\(\Delta t\)), living agents are activated. Notice that we randomly shuffle the execution order (sample.int(...)) so that no individual cell receives an unfair “first-move” advantage at every turn:

model_step <- function(m) {
  # Asynchronous random scheduling: shuffle living agent indices
  ids <- which(m$alive)
  ids <- ids[sample.int(length(ids))]
  
  for (i in ids) {
    if (!m$alive[i]) next
    if (m$type[i] == TUMOUR) tumour_step(m, i) else tcell_step(m, i)
  }
  
  nutrient_step(m)   # Regenerate depleted vascular nutrients
  recruit_tcells(m)  # Recruit circulating T cells based on tumour antigen load
  m$step <- m$step + 1L
  record(m)          # Record population census

  # Housekeeping: periodically prune dead agents from memory so vectors stay compact
  if (length(m$alive) > 4 * sum(m$alive) + 500) {
    keep <- m$alive
    old_ids <- which(keep)
    for (v in c("type", "x", "y", "energy", "kills", "alive")) m[[v]] <- m[[v]][keep]
    remap <- integer(length(keep))
    remap[old_ids] <- seq_along(old_ids)
    m$tum[m$tum > 0L] <- remap[m$tum[m$tum > 0L]]
  }
}

6.4.4.1 1. Interactive 2D Spatial Lattice (plot_grid)

The spatial microenvironment is visualized by layering an interactive heatmap of vascular nutrient availability beneath scatter markers for tumour cells (purple), active T cells (blue), and exhausted T cells (grey).

# Color palette for intuitive biological distinction

col_active    <- "#1F77B4"  # Vibrant blue for active cytotoxic T cells

plot_grid <- function(m) {
  W <- m$W; H <- m$H; R <- m$p$resupply_time
  level <- ifelse(m$fully, 1, 1 - m$countdown / R)
  lim <- exh_limit(m)
  tu  <- which(m$alive & m$type == TUMOUR)
  tc  <- which(m$alive & m$type == TCELL)
  ex  <- tc[m$kills[tc] >= lim]
  ac  <- tc[m$kills[tc] < lim]

  # Background heatmap: vascular nutrient replenishment level
  p <- plot_ly() |>
    add_heatmap(
      x = seq_len(W), y = seq_len(H), z = t(level),
      zmin = 0, zmax = 1, showscale = FALSE,
      colorscale = list(c(0, "#5A3A2E"), c(1, "#F3DCD4")),
      hovertemplate = "site (%{x}, %{y})<br>nutrient resupplied: %{z:.0%}<extra></extra>"
    )

  # Scatter overlay: Tumour cells
  if (length(tu) > 0) {
    p <- p |> add_markers(
      x = m$x[tu], y = m$y[tu], name = "Tumour cells",
      marker = list(symbol = "circle", size = 7, color = col_tumour,
                    line = list(color = "white", width = 0.5)),
      text = sprintf("Tumour cell<br>energy: %.1f", m$energy[tu]),
      hoverinfo = "text"
    )
  }
  
  # Scatter overlay: Active Cytotoxic T cells
  if (length(ac) > 0) {
    p <- p |> add_markers(
      x = m$x[ac] + 0.2, y = m$y[ac] + 0.2 * runif(length(ac)), name = "Active T cells",
      marker = list(symbol = "triangle-up", size = 9, color = col_active,
                    line = list(color = "white", width = 0.8)),
      text = sprintf("Active T cell<br>energy: %.1f<br>kills: %d", m$energy[ac], m$kills[ac]),
      hoverinfo = "text"
    )
  }
  
  # Scatter overlay: Exhausted T cells
  if (length(ex) > 0) {
    p <- p |> add_markers(
      x = m$x[ex] + 0.2, y = m$y[ex] + 0.2 * runif(length(ex)), name = "Exhausted T cells",
      marker = list(symbol = "triangle-up", size = 9, color = col_exhausted,
                    line = list(color = "white", width = 0.8)),
      text = sprintf("Exhausted T cell<br>energy: %.1f<br>kills: %d", m$energy[ex], m$kills[ex]),
      hoverinfo = "text"
    )
  }

  status <- if (!m$p$therapy) "" else if (therapy_on(m)) "  |  therapy ON" else
    sprintf("  |  therapy at step %d", m$p$therapy_start)

  p |>
    layout(
      title = list(
        text = sprintf("Step %d  |  tumour: %d  T cells: %d (%d exhausted)%s",
                       m$step, length(tu), length(tc), length(ex), status),
        font = list(size = 13)
      ),

                   title = "", constrain = "domain"),
      yaxis = list(range = c(0.5, H + 0.5), showgrid = FALSE, zeroline = FALSE,
                   title = "", scaleanchor = "x", constrain = "domain"),
      legend = list(orientation = "h", y = -0.05),
      margin = list(t = 40, l = 10, r = 10, b = 10)
    )
}

plot_timeseries <- function(m) {

plot_animated_simulation <- function(m, n_steps = 30) {
  records <- list()
  
  for (s in 1:n_steps) {
    if (s > 1) model_step(m)
    
    tu  <- which(m$alive & m$type == TUMOUR)
    tc  <- which(m$alive & m$type == TCELL)

      info = c("", "", ""),
      step = s
    )
    records[[length(records) + 1]] <- dummy_df
    
    if (length(tu) > 0) {

                           levels = c("Tumour cells", "Active T cells", "Exhausted T cells")),
        info = sprintf("Tumour cell<br>Energy: %.1f", m$energy[tu]),
        step = s
      )
    }
    if (length(ac) > 0) {
      records[[length(records) + 1]] <- data.frame(

        cell_type = factor(rep("Active T cells", length(ac)),
                           levels = c("Tumour cells", "Active T cells", "Exhausted T cells")),
        info = sprintf("Active T cell<br>Energy: %.1f<br>Kills: %d", m$energy[ac], m$kills[ac]),
        step = s
      )
    }
    if (length(ex) > 0) {
      records[[length(records) + 1]] <- data.frame(
        x = m$x[ex] + 0.15, y = m$y[ex] + 0.15,
        cell_type = factor(rep("Exhausted T cells", length(ex)),
                           levels = c("Tumour cells", "Active T cells", "Exhausted T cells")),
        info = sprintf("Exhausted T cell<br>Energy: %.1f<br>Kills: %d", m$energy[ex], m$kills[ex]),
        step = s
      )
    }
  }
  
  df_anim <- do.call(rbind, records)
  
  plot_ly(
    df_anim,
    x = ~x, y = ~y, color = ~cell_type,
    colors = c("Tumour cells" = col_tumour, "Active T cells" = col_active, "Exhausted T cells" = col_exhausted),
    frame = ~step,
    type = "scatter", mode = "markers",
    marker = list(size = 8, opacity = 0.85, line = list(color = "white", width = 0.5)),
    text = ~info, hoverinfo = "text"
  ) |>
    layout(
      title = list(text = "Interactive Spatial Simulation (Click Play or Drag Slider)", font = list(size = 14)),
      xaxis = list(range = c(0.5, m$W + 0.5), title = "Lattice X", showgrid = FALSE, zeroline = FALSE, constrain = "domain"),
      yaxis = list(range = c(0.5, m$H + 0.5), title = "Lattice Y", showgrid = FALSE, zeroline = FALSE, scaleanchor = "x", constrain = "domain"),
      legend = list(orientation = "h", y = -0.15),
      margin = list(t = 40, l = 10, r = 10, b = 40)
    ) |>
    animation_opts(frame = 250, transition = 0, redraw = FALSE) |>

}

6.4.5 Exploring Biological Regimes: The Three “E”s of Cancer Immunoediting

In modern oncology, the interaction between tumours and the immune system is categorized by the Three “E”s of Cancer Immunoediting:

  1. Elimination: The nascent tumor is successfully recognized and eradicated by cytotoxic immune responses.
  2. Equilibrium: Tumour cells and immune cells reach a dynamic standoff, keeping the tumor burden stable over extended time scales (the spatial analog of stable Lotka-Volterra coexistence).
  3. Escape: Persistent stimulation triggers immune exhaustion and suppresses cytotoxicity, enabling the tumor to break through immune control.

6.4.5.1 Scenario 1: Stable Immune Equilibrium

Under our default parameters, the tumor nodule expands until systemic recruitment and chemotaxis mobilize cytotoxic T cells to the tumor boundary, producing a stable standoff:

# Initialize the model with default equilibrium parameters
sim_equilibrium <- initialize_model(default_params)

# Render the interactive in-browser animated player over 25 steps
plot_animated_simulation(sim_equilibrium, n_steps = 25)

Now let us run the model forward for an additional \(75\) steps (totaling \(100\) steps) and examine the macroscopic population trajectories:

# Advance simulation to step 100
for (step in 26:100) {
  model_step(sim_equilibrium)
}

# Render final 2D spatial microenvironment snapshot
plot_grid(sim_equilibrium)
# Render interactive macroscopic population trajectories
plot_timeseries(sim_equilibrium)

6.4.5.2 Scenario 2: Immune Escape and Checkpoint Inhibitor Rescue

What happens if T-cell exhaustion occurs very rapidly (exhaustion_limit = 2)? The tumor quickly exhausts its attackers and breaks free (immune escape). However, administering checkpoint inhibitor therapy (anti-PD-1) at step \(120\) quadruples the exhaustion limit and boosts killing efficiency, rejuvenating exhausted cells and controlling the escaping tumor.

# Configure scenario: low exhaustion limit, with therapy starting at step 120
escape_params <- default_params
escape_params$exhaustion_limit   <- 2       # Rapid exhaustion triggers immune escape
escape_params$therapy            <- TRUE    # Administer checkpoint inhibitor
escape_params$therapy_start      <- 120     # Therapy initiated at step 120
escape_params$therapy_kill_boost <- 1.6     # 60% boost to cytotoxicity
escape_params$therapy_exh_mult   <- 4       # Quadruple exhaustion limit

sim_therapy <- initialize_model(escape_params)

# Simulate 220 time steps across the pre-treatment and post-treatment phases
for (step in 1:220) {
  model_step(sim_therapy)
}

# Render interactive time series showing tumor escape and therapeutic rescue
plot_timeseries(sim_therapy)

Notice how the purple tumour curve initially skyrockets between steps \(40\) and \(120\) as active T cells plummet into exhaustion (the dashed grey curve). The moment checkpoint inhibitor therapy is administered at step \(120\) (indicated by the vertical green marker), exhausted T cells are rapidly reinvigorated, driving tumour eradication!
